# Precedent: Force Majeure — COVID-19 and Pandemic Events

**Status:** Approved precedent  
**Applicable to:** Supply agreements, service agreements  
**Jurisdiction:** Germany (DE) primary; notes on UK and US  
**Last reviewed:** 2024-Q1

---

## Background

During 2020–2022, BMW encountered numerous contractual disputes regarding force majeure claims by suppliers citing COVID-19 disruptions. This precedent summarizes how German courts and BMW's legal team approached these claims.

---

## German Law Position (BGB § 275, § 313)

### § 275 BGB — Impossibility

Courts have held (BGH, confirmed by lower courts) that **COVID-19 supply chain disruption generally does not constitute legal impossibility** under § 275 BGB because:
- Performance is typically *more difficult* or *more expensive*, not impossible
- Economic hardship is not impossibility under German law
- BGH case law requires that performance become objectively impossible (not just more burdensome)

**Source:** BGH confirmed this principle across multiple 2021–2022 decisions. See also OLG Frankfurt, Urteil v. 28.01.2021 – 32 U 67/20.

### § 313 BGB — Adjustment for Changed Circumstances (Störung der Geschäftsgrundlage)

More relevant is § 313 BGB (frustration of contract basis):
- Requires: (1) fundamental change of circumstances; (2) parties would not have concluded contract on same terms; (3) adherence to contract is unreasonable
- Courts applied this narrowly: **general market disruption does not trigger § 313** unless parties specifically contracted around pandemic risk
- Price increases of 20–40% were held insufficient to trigger § 313 (OLG Köln, several 2021 decisions)

## BMW Standard Clause Language

### Force Majeure Definition (Current BMW Standard)

> "Force Majeure Event means any event or circumstance beyond a party's reasonable control that prevents or materially impedes performance, including but not limited to: acts of God, war, terrorism, civil unrest, government orders or sanctions, and natural disasters. For the avoidance of doubt, Force Majeure Events do not include: (a) economic or market conditions, including raw material price increases; (b) labour disputes within a party's own workforce; (c) events that were foreseeable at the time of contract formation; or (d) events that could have been prevented by reasonable precautions."

### Pandemic-Specific Carve-out (Post-2020 BMW Addition)

> "Supply chain disruptions caused by pandemics, epidemics, or public health emergencies declared by a competent governmental authority may constitute Force Majeure Events only if: (i) the disruption directly prevents the affected party from obtaining materials or performing services required under this Agreement; and (ii) the affected party has exhausted commercially reasonable alternative sources and mitigation measures."

## UK Law Position

Under English law, force majeure is entirely contractual (no statutory equivalent to § 275/313 BGB). COVID-19 invocations were assessed based on exact clause wording. Courts (English High Court, 2021–2022) generally required:
- "Natural disaster" language was held NOT to cover pandemic by several courts
- Explicit "epidemic" or "pandemic" language was necessary for coverage

**Key case:** *Tandrin Aviation Holdings Ltd v Aero Toy Store LLC* [2010] EWHC 40 Comm (pre-COVID, but established that economic downturns are not force majeure under standard English clauses — applied to COVID disputes by analogy).

---

## Recommended Contract Language Going Forward

For all supply agreements, include:

```
FORCE MAJEURE
[BMW Standard definition above, including pandemic carve-out]

For COVID-19 and equivalent public health emergencies: Relief is available only upon: 
(i) prompt written notice within 5 business days of the event;
(ii) written evidence of governmental restriction directly causing inability to perform;
(iii) demonstration that alternative performance options have been exhausted;
(iv) mitigation plan with projected recovery timeline.
```

---

## Internal Guidance (BMW Legal Department)

- **Do not accept** force majeure clauses that define "pandemic" or "epidemic" without the mitigation and foreseeability requirements above
- **Require** the 5-business-day notice requirement as a condition of force majeure protection
- **Negotiate** that force majeure suspends (not extinguishes) obligations, and include a termination right after 60 days
